However, their effectiveness in medical applications remains limited due to the inherent discrepancies between data and tasks in medical scenarios and those in the general domain. Existing medical MLLMs face the following critical deficiencies: 1) inadequate coverage of medical knowledge beyond imaging; 2) elevated propensity for hallucinations due to suboptimal data curation; and 3) limited reasoning capacity tailored to complex medical tasks. I Introduction Recent advancements in Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) have significantly propelled progress in multimodal domains [ 1 , 2 , 3 ] , demonstrating capabilities that approach expert-level proficiency in areas such as image captioning, visual question answering, and complex reasoning. Despite these successes, the performance of MLLMs in medical applications remains notably constrained [ 4 , 5 ] . Biomedical image-text pairs differ fundamentally from typical web-based content, presenting unique challenges that general visual assistants are not well-equipped to handle [ 6 ] . Lacking domain-specific medical visual knowledge, these models often respond to biomedical inquiries with uncertainty or, worse, with incorrect information and hallucinations [ 7 , 8 ] . To address these gaps, recent studies have adapted models for medical contexts by integrating general-purpose MLLMs with medical multimodal data to build specialized models [ 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 ] . These models demonstrate encouraging results in medical visual question answering (VQA), report generation, and diagnostic support, underscoring the potential of MLLMs in healthcare scenarios. Pioneering models, such as LLaVA-Med [ 7 ] , directly employ PubMed-derived datasets for vision-language alignment and fine-tuning. However, their performance remains limited due to low data quality and high levels of noise within PubMed. Subsequent research [ 8 , 6 , 13 ] addresses the challenges with refined training recipes and larger, higher-quality medical datasets. Concurrently, advances in reasoning models, exemplified by OpenAI o-series 2 2 2 https://openai.com/index/introducing-o3-and-o4-mini/ and DeepSeek-R1 [ 14 ] , set new benchmarks through advanced post-training techniques. Inspired by reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR [ 15 ] ), some studies [ 16 , 17 ] integrate it into medical models to improve reasoning and enhance performance in clinical applications. Despite significant progress, existing medical MLLMs face four critical limitations that fundamentally constrain their clinical utility: First, inadequate medical knowledge coverage beyond imaging . Existing approaches typically focus on distilling image-text pairs from advanced models [ 1 , 2 ] . This approach is scalable, but image-based prompts fail to elicit crucial non-visual medical knowledge in pharmacology, public health, and clinical contexts from advanced models. This narrow focus on visual modalities creates knowledge gaps that limit performance on tasks requiring comprehensive medical understanding. Second, increasing hallucinations without proper data quality control. Many distillation methods rely solely on model outputs without additional supervision, which increases the risk of generating false information. Third, lack of systematic training strategies and data composition strategies for medical MLLMs. Most existing works adopt ad-hoc approaches: directly fine-tuning general-purpose MLLMs with all available medical multimodal data [ 8 , 13 ] . However, critical questions remain unexplored: when to introduce different data types , what each component contributes , and whether non-medical data benefits medical training . This lack of principled investigation results in suboptimal protocols that fail to effectively leverage available data. In this work, we develop our medical MLLMs Lingshu-Med 3 3 3 Lingshu-Med originates from a chapter title of Huangdi Neijing ( https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huangdi_Neijing ), one of the earliest classical texts of traditional Chinese medicine. . Lingshu-Med addresses the above limitations by: 1) a comprehensive data curation framework that efficiently acquires medical knowledge from diverse sources: not only medical multimodal data but also extensive medical texts and general-domain data; 2) A robust data synthesizing pipeline for generating captions, QA pairs, and chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning samples with rigorous quality control; and 3) systematic investigation of training strategies and data composition through comprehensive ablation studies, based on which we design a principled three-stage training paradigm that progressively infuses medical knowledge while preserving general capabilities.
We also investigate RLVR for enhancing multimodal medical reasoning. While our initial exploration does not yield significant improvements, we conduct a thorough analysis to identify key challenges and provide valuable insights for future work. In addition, we find that current models are often evaluated in isolated, non-standardized environments. The lack of accessible and unified evaluation frameworks further hinders the development of the medical field. Therefore, we introduce MedEvalKit, a unified framework that consolidates the major multimodal and textual medical benchmarks for an efficient, standardized model assessment. Extensive experiments demonstrate that Lingshu-Med consistently achieves SOTA performance across most multimodal and textual medical VQA tasks, as well as in report generation, in both 7B and 32B configurations. Notably, in medical VQA, Lingshu-Med-32B outperforms the second-best model by an average of 7.2 7.2 accuracy points across seven tasks, even surpassing proprietary models like GPT-4.1 and Claude Sonnet 4. Through comprehensive analyses, we reveal critical insights for medical MLLM development: (1) medical textual data and general-domain data contain essential medical knowledge absent in multimodal medical datasets, significantly improving knowledge coverage and downstream performance; (2) data with high logical complexity substantially enhances medical reasoning capabilities; (3) early-stage training on medical captions, despite temporarily sacrificing instruction-following ability, establishes a stronger foundation that enables higher performance ceilings in later stages. Finally, the case study underscores the great potential of Lingshu-Med across numerous medical applications, including medical report generation, medical consultation, and clinical surgery assistance. In summary, our contributions are fourfold: 1) Data : We introduce a novel data curation pipeline that efficiently gathers medical knowledge from various resources and synthesizes high-quality medical captions, QA pairs, and CoT reasoning samples; 2) Training : We conduct systematic investigations of training strategies and data composition for medical MLLMs through comprehensive ablation studies, revealing insights into optimal inclusion and integration of training data. Based on these findings, we propose Lingshu-Med, medical-specific MLLMs in 7B and 32B sizes, trained with a principled three-stage paradigm that progressively infuses medical knowledge; 3) Evaluation : We propose MedEvalKit, a unified evaluation framework that consolidates major benchmarks for multimodal and textual medical tasks, streamlining model assessment and advancing standardized performance evaluation in the medical field; 4) Performance : Through rigorous experimental validations, Lingshu-Med demonstrates state-of-the-art performance across multiple multimodal and textual medical QA tasks, as well as in radiology report generation. II Data Curation We address the intricate challenges of medical data curation via two primary strategies: 1) a comprehensive collection of diverse medical knowledge sources, including medical multimodal datasets, extensive medical texts, and general-domain multimodal and textual data; and 2) the synthesis of high-quality medical data through a robust pipeline capable of generating detailed captions, OCR-based samples, QA pairs, and chain-of-thought reasoning examples. The overall medical data curation pipeline is illustrated in Fig. 2 . For data collection , we curate a wide range of open-source datasets from the web, categorized as follows: 1) multimodal medical data for enhancing Lingshu-Med’s comprehension of medical images and downstream task performance; 2) unimodal medical data , including standalone medical images and texts, to enrich domain knowledge and support synthetic data generation; 3) general-domain data to improve general visual and language understanding. All data undergo strict quality filtering before use. Each dataset is individually preprocessed and cleaned to ensure the quality and relevance. For medical data synthesis , we generate targeted synthetic data to strengthen specific model capabilities: 1) long-form captions to improve diagnostic detail extraction from images; 2) OCR-based instruction data to enhance text recognition in medical imagery; 3) VQA samples for tasks such as diagnosis, anatomical recognition, and modality classification; and 4) distilled reasoning trajectories to promote advanced medical reasoning. All synthesized data are subject to a rigorous quality control process to ensure accuracy and reliability. II-A Data Collection II-A 1 Medical Multimodal Data Collection We incorporate two primary categories of existing multimodal medical data into our training set: 1) medical caption data , which pairs one or more medical images with descriptive text to facilitate semantic alignment between visual and textual modalities; and 2) medical multimodal instruction data , which includes image-instruction pairs. The latter comprises two main types: medical VQA data , featuring open-ended or multiple-choice questions about medical images, and medical report data , consisting of radiology reports that detail clinical findings and impressions. These datasets support MLLMs in learning a broad range of medical multimodal tasks. II-A 2 Medical Unimodal Data Collection Medical text instruction data. We incorporate medical text instruction data into our training corpus to enhance the domain knowledge of Lingshu-Med. The data spans four categories: 1) medical factoid QA , including both free-form and multiple-choice questions on medical knowledge; 2) distilled reasoning data , which supplements answers with step-by-step reasoning path annotated by large reasoning models; 3) patient-doctor dialogues , comprising single-turn exchanges with patient queries and corresponding doctor responses; and 4) general medical instruction data , covering diverse tasks such as medication summarization and medical text translation. Medical imaging data. It comprises raw images paired with human-annotated metadata, including depicted organs, diagnostic labels, and imaging modalities. This metadata enables the synthesis of additional captioning and VQA samples, thereby improving medical image understanding and performance of downstream tasks. We primarily use these datasets for data synthesis (as detailed in § II-B ). The complete lists of the collected datasets for medical multimodal and unimodal data are provided in Appendix § A1 . II-A 3 General Domain Data Collection To enhance the general multimodal understanding, generation, and instruction-following abilities of Lingshu-Med, we incorporate diverse general-domain datasets, including image captioning, textual instruction-following, and multimodal instruction-following data, which collectively enhance the model’s generalization to a wide range of vision-language tasks. Specifically, we utilize LLaVA-1.5 captions [ 18 ] and PixMo [ 19 ] for image captioning; LLaVA-1.5 (textual instruction subset) and OpenHermes-2.5 4 4 4 https://huggingface.co/datasets/teknium/OpenHermes-2.5 for textual instruction-following; and LLaVA-1.5 (multimodal instruction subset), along with ALLaVA [ 20 ] for multimodal instruction-following. II-A 4 Data Cleaning Process Data cleaning for medical multimodal data. Many open-source multimodal medical datasets are automatically extracted from scientific papers and thus often contain noise and redundancy. To ensure data quality, we implement a three-stage cleaning pipeline: 1) image filtering , where images with dimensions smaller than 64 64 pixels are removed to eliminate low-resolution or uninformative content; 2) image deduplication , which applies perceptual hashing to remove exact duplicate images; 3) text filtering , which filters out lengthy and potentially noisy examples. Further details are provided in Appendix § A2 . Data cleaning for medical text data. To ensure the quality and compliance of the collected medical text instruction data, we implement two key cleaning procedures. First, we apply model-based response cleaning to remove sensitive identifying information and explicit prescriptions from the responses in patient-doctor dialogue datasets, thereby mitigating privacy and legal risks. Second, to eliminate redundancy, we perform text deduplication across multiple instruction datasets using MinHash locality-sensitive hashing (LSH). Implementation details are provided in Appendix § A2 . Fig. 2 : The overall data curation pipeline of medical multimodal and textual data. II-B Medical Data Synthesis This section details our methodologies for synthesizing four types of medical multimodal data—long-form captions, OCR-based instruction samples, VQA instances, and distilled reasoning examples—alongside the quality control strategies employed to ensure their accuracy and reliability. II-B 1 Medical Long-form Caption Synthesis Given the limited descriptive depth of existing medical captions, we construct enriched captions that capture key visual features of medical images. To this end, we utilize data from medical image segmentation and classification tasks, including human-annotated disease regions, diagnostic labels, and other visible metadata ( e.g . , gender and camera view). This factual knowledge enables the synthesis of authentic and detailed medical captions. To ensure the model’s visual perception across imaging modalities, we source data from multiple modalities and balance their volume. Construction details are provided in § A6 . In the following, we show the key methods. Specifically, for each image, we employ GPT-4o [ 1 ] to generate a long-form caption that captures fine-grained visual features. We devise the following five-stage pipeline to synthesize approximately 100K high-quality medical image captions. 1) Metadata preparation: We leverage metadata to provide essential contextual information for a given image, including the imaging modality, type of disease, and other observable characteristics of the patient. Following Xie et al. [ 21 ] , we extract metadata from each dataset and apply dataset-specific rules to convert it into short captions. 2) RoI identification: In addition to the textual metadata, we incorporate complementary visual information. For image segmentation datasets, they provide the location of the abnormality through RoIs, which are critical to reduce hallucinations and must be clearly marked in the images. We apply different ways to overlay RoIs on the source images. 3) Annotation with factual knowledge: We prompt GPT-4o to synthesize image descriptions by integrating textual and visual information from the previous two stages. 4) Annotation with doctor preference: Although the generated factual descriptions are highly reliable, they are often constrained by explicitly available metadata and may overlook critical visual details beyond RoIs. To mitigate this, we consult medical professionals to identify key factors they use to interpret medical images. Their insights are distilled into task-specific instructions for prompt GPT-4o. 5) Summarization: We use GPT-4o to combine the outputs of stages 3 and 4 to consolidate the final caption for each medical image. To avoid conflict, the description from stage 3 is prioritized due to its higher reliability. Quality control: To ensure caption quality, we implement an iterative validation process with medical professionals. We progressively annotate small batches of samples of all modalities and have doctors review them for accuracy and clinical relevance. Based on their feedback, we refine our annotation pipeline and prompts. Only after obtaining approval from medical experts on the quality of these pilot annotations do we proceed with large-scale captioning. This iterative approach ensures that our final captions meet clinical standards before scaling up the annotation process. Appendix § A4 presents an example of a synthesized long-form caption for an MRI image. II-B 2 Medical OCR-based Instruction Data Synthesis OCR-based instruction datasets enhance the model’s ability to interpret textual information embedded within medical images, a common scenario involving elements such as patient identifiers, age, and biological metrics. We collect biology and chemistry exam questions with ground-truth answers from online sources, as these subjects are closely related to the medical domain and offer rich resources. Each question is annotated with detailed reasoning steps using Gemini-2.0-Flash-Thinking [ 2 ] , and only samples with answers that exactly match the ground truth are retained. The validated questions are then rendered as images to form multimodal instruction data, where the question image serves as input and the textual answer as output (see Table V in Appendix § A4 ). This process yields a total of 50K high-quality OCR-based samples. II-B 3 Medical VQA Data Synthesis Medical diagnosis, anatomy identification, and modality classification are core competencies required for MLLM to perform various multimodal medical tasks. To strengthen Lingshu-Med’s capabilities in these areas, we synthesize additional medical VQA data using two complementary strategies: a template-based method and a self-instruct-based method. Template-based method. This method constructs VQA samples using human-annotated meta-information, e.g . , abnormality labels, from public medical imaging datasets. Specifically, we extract datasets containing labels related to anatomical structures, abnormalities, or imaging modalities. For each label type, we manually design question templates. Ground-truth answers are derived from the original annotations, while distractor options are sampled from the label space of the respective dataset. This approach enables the structured generation of high-quality VQA data. Self-instruct-based method. To enhance question diversity and linguistic variation, we leverage GPT-4o to generate VQA samples from (image, caption) pairs sourced from medical captioning datasets. GPT-4o is prompted using a few-shot format, where seed examples—drawn from open-source medical VQA datasets—demonstrate questions focused on diagnosis and anatomy. It then produces a question, options, and the correct answer based on the provided caption to enable more varied and natural question formulations. The overall pipeline of both template-based and self-instruct-based methods and the prompt design of the self-instruct-based method are detailed in Appendix § A4 . Fig. 3 : The distribution of medical image modality in our training data (medical subset). II-B 4 Medical Reasoning Data Distillation Most existing open-source and synthesized medical data, primarily composed of short-answer and multiple-choice questions, lack explicit annotations of the underlying reasoning process. To strengthen the model’s medical reasoning capabilities, we employ GPT-4o to generate chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning trajectories for a subset of both multimodal and textual instruction data. Specifically, for each sample, we provide GPT-4o with the question, ground-truth answer, and, if applicable, the answer options, and the corresponding medical image. GPT-4o is instructed to produce a step-by-step reasoning path without relying on or explicitly referencing the ground-truth answer. To ensure quality, we implement an LLM-based validation process wherein GPT-4o evaluates the consistency between the reasoning trace and the ground-truth answer. Samples deemed inconsistent are excluded from the final dataset. The prompts are detailed in Appendix § A5 . II-C Dataset Summary The data collection and synthesis pipelines yield 3.75M open-source and 1.30M synthetic medical samples, respectively. We also conduct rigorous quality checks on all samples to ensure overall data quality. To prevent data contamination, we perform strict image and text deduplication by excluding images and samples that overlap with the evaluation benchmarks in MedEvalKit . Moreover, we examine the distribution of medical imaging modalities within the training set. As many multimodal datasets lack explicit modality annotations, we use a BiomedCLIP-based classifier [ 22 ] to infer the modalities of unlabeled samples. Fig. 3 presents the resulting modality distribution. Our dataset covers over 12 medical imaging modalities, substantially enhancing the model’s versatility and applicability on medical downstream tasks across a wide spectrum of modalities. III Model Training Lingshu-Med is built upon the Qwen2.5-VL [ 23 ] , consisting of three key components: an LLM, a vision encoder, and an MLP-based projector. We employ two parameter scales of Qwen2.5-VL, i.e . , 7B and 32B, as the foundational models. Building on prior MLLM advances [ 23 , 3 ] , we develop a three-stage training framework following a “shallow-to-deep” progression: 1) Medical Shallow Alignment , 2) Medical Deep Alignment , and 3) Medical Instruction Tuning , as shown in Fig. 4 . The first two stages build a solid medical vision-language foundation. Medical Shallow Alignment utilizes a small set of medical captions for basic visual understanding. Medical Deep Alignment uses larger and semantically richer datasets to deepen domain knowledge and achieve finer vision-language alignment. The later stages enhance practical utility. Medical Instruction Tuning improves task-specific instruction following across medical use cases. Fig. 4 : The training pipeline of Lingshu-Med: medical shallow alignment, medical deep alignment, and medical instruction tuning. III-A Medical Shallow Alignment This stage aims to establish an effective alignment between diverse medical imaging modalities and their corresponding descriptions. This alignment enhances the model’s ability to comprehend and interpret medical images, thereby laying a solid foundation for subsequent integration of medical knowledge. During this phase, the LLM is kept frozen, while only the vision encoder and the projector are trained using coarsely annotated medical image-caption data. We deliberately freeze the LLM because the limited semantic complexity of coarse caption data could potentially compromise the language generation capabilities of the LLM. Specifically, the coarse caption data comprise two datasets with relatively short captions, i.e . , PMC-OA [ 24 ] and ROCO [ 25 ] . III-B Medical Deep Alignment The goal of medical deep alignment is to comprehensively integrate medical knowledge into the MLLM, enhancing its ability to understand diverse medical concepts and adapt to various clinical contexts. To achieve this, all model parameters are unfrozen to allow end-to-end fine-tuning. Compared to the preceding shallow alignment stage, the dataset in this stage is significantly expanded in terms of modality diversity, linguistic complexity, and structural completeness. It includes images from less common medical modalities, longer and more structured captions, as well as image-caption pairs synthesized from medical image classification and segmentation tasks. Moreover, high-quality general-domain multimodal image-text data are incorporated into the training process alongside medical data. This joint training not only helps preserve the MLLM’s general multimodal capabilities but also introduces it to a wider variety of visual formats, such as charts, tables, graphs, mathematics, and scientific illustrations. III-C Medical Instruction Tuning Following standard practices [ 7 , 8 , 26 ] , we fine-tune the MLLM for instruction following across various clinical tasks. To accommodate a wide breadth of clinical scenarios, we unlock all parameters for training and leverage a comprehensive training corpus that goes beyond basic formats like image captions and QA pairs. Our data include comprehensive medical scenarios spanning diagnosis, clinical examination, knowledge retrieval, report generation, and anatomical localization. We also add carefully vetted general-domain and medical text data to reduce image-centric bias and to improve overall medical understanding. The corpus also includes complex formats such as multi-image reasoning, multi-turn dialogues, and detailed reasoning tasks, enabling the MLLM to handle clinical scenarios requiring greater analytical capabilities. IV Unified Medical Evaluation Framework (a) Medical Multimodal Benchmark (b) Medical Text-only Benchmark (c) Report Gen. Benchmark Fig. 5 : An overview of the data distribution across benchmarks for different types of medical tasks. Rapid progress of medical MLLMs has yielded impressive performance across various tasks. However, the absence of a unified evaluation framework has led to inconsistencies in performance comparison across models. While existing frameworks like LMMs-Eval [ 27 ] , Eval-Harness, 5 5 5 https://zenodo.org/records/12608602 and VLMEvalKit [ 28 ] can be applied to evaluate medical MLLMs, they are primarily designed for general tasks. Thus, they exhibit limitations in medical data coverage and model adaptation, making them difficult to apply directly. To comprehensively evaluate the performance of medical MLLMs, we develop a systematic evaluation framework, dubbed MedEvalKit, which integrates mainstream medical benchmarks and task types and supports a variety of question formats, including multiple-choice, closed-ended, open-ended questions, as well as medical report generation. It covers representative tasks in medical multimodal and textual understanding, thereby offering broad applicability. MedEvalKit accommodates both multimodal and text-only inputs, enabling a unified evaluation of both medical MLLMs and LLMs. To enhance reproducibility, we standardize data preprocessing formats and post-processing protocols. MedEvalKit also incorporates a dual verification mechanism that combines rule-based evaluation with the LLM-as-a-Judge strategy, integrating both objective and subjective assessments to improve evaluation stability and reliability of the evaluation. Additionally, MedEvalKit supports inference acceleration via vLLM [ 29 ] , enabling high-throughput and parallel evaluation, and offering strong scalability and engineering usability. IV-A A Large-scale Medical Evaluation Benchmark We curate a collection of mainstream medical benchmarks covering multimodal QA, text-only QA, and report generation, comprising a total of 152,066 samples with 121,622 distinct medical images spanning 16 benchmark datasets. An overview of the medical benchmark is depicted in Fig. 5 . Moreover, we standardize the input format for all questions based on their types, adhering to the official chat template of medical MLLM candidates to be evaluated. More details of the benchmark and prompt format are in Appendix § D . Multimodal QA contains the test sets from VQA-RAD [ 30 ] , SLAKE [ 31 ] , PathVQA [ 32 ] , PMC-VQA (v2; [ 11 ] ), OmniMedVQA [ 33 ] , MMMU [ 34 ] , and MedXpertQA [ 35 ] . Specifically, we select the Health&Medical subset of MMMU, multimodal set of MedXpertQA, and open-access part of OmniMedVQA. By incorporating these datasets, MedEvalKit encompasses various medical imaging modalities, including X-ray, CT, MRI, PET, ultrasound, microscopy, pathology, OCT, dermoscopy, gastrointestinal, endoscopy, fundus, and medical-related charts, tables, and figures. Text-only QA covers multiple medical text benchmarks, including the test sets of MedQA-USMLE ( [ 36 ] ), MMLU-Med [ 37 ] , PubMedQA [ 38 ] , MedMCQA [ 39 ] , MedBullets [ 40 ] , MedXpertQA [ 35 ] , and SuperGPQA [ 41 ] . We select the text portion of MedXpertQA, the medicine-related questions of MMLU by following the split in Wang et al. [ 42 ] , and the test set of SuperGPQA by the official split criteria. Report Generation contains the official test splits of MIMIC-CXR, 6 6 6 https://physionet.org/content/mimic-cxr/2.1.0/ IU-Xray, 7 7 7 https://openi.nlm.nih.gov/faq?download=true and the validation set of CheXpert Plus, 8 8 8 https://aimi.stanford.edu/datasets/chexpert-plus which consist of chest X-rays paired with corresponding radiology reports. We follow Zhang et al. [ 43 ] to filter out samples where both findings and impressions are empty. IV-B Evaluation Metrics We use Accuracy as the primary metric for the QA task. For multiple-choice questions , a two-stage strategy is adopted: rule-based matching is first applied to assess alignment with answer options; if this is insufficient, we further employ the official MMMU codebase 9 9 9 https://github.com/MMMU-Benchmark/MMMU to compute similarity scores and select the option with the highest score as the final answer. For open-ended questions , we directly leverage GPT-4.1 10 10 10 https://openai.com/index/gpt-4-1/ to assess consistency between model outputs and reference answers, with the prompting strategy detailed in Appendix § D . As GPT-4.1 also appears as a compared model in our benchmark tables, the LLM judge receives only the question, the reference answer, and the candidate output, with all model identities withheld. Candidate outputs are normalized to a consistent textual format ( e.g . , the choice index “C” for multi-choice QA) before judging. It is therefore blind to which model produced each answer. To verify the reliability of the automatic judge, we conducted a human spot-check on 100 randomly sampled responses, on which the GPT-4.1 verdicts agreed with human
annotators in 98% of cases. For the report generation task, we develop a multi-metric evaluation approach that integrates semantic and model-based metrics, specifically tailored to address the challenges posed by the lengthy and highly open-ended nature of the outputs. This design enables a more comprehensive and granular assessment of the performance of model . For the semantic evaluation metrics, we choose Rouge-L [ 44 ] and CIDEr [ 45 ] to measure the quality of model-generated reports with respect to reference answers. For the model-based evaluation metrics, we follow ReXrank [ 43 ] and utilize SembScore [ 46 ] and RaTEScore [ 47 ] to measure the candidate reports. We also include a composite metric RadCliQ v1 [ 48 ] . As lower RadCliQ v1 scores indicate better performance, we follow Zhang et al. [ 43 ] to take its reciprocal, i.e . , RadCliQ − 1 v ​ 1 {}_{v1}^{-1} . Detailed descriptions of these metrics are presented in Appendix § D . We note that automatic report-generation metrics primarily capture surface-level overlap and entity matching, and may not fully reflect factual correctness. We therefore complement them with the physician study in §V-F, where the Safety, Completeness, and Clinical Relevance dimensions directly assess hallucinated content, clinically significant omissions, and factual correctness of the generated reports. V Experiments V-A Experimental Settings In the main paper, we primarily implement Lingshu-Med pipeline using Qwen2.5-VL. To demonstrate its generalizability, we also evaluate the pipeline on the recent InternVL3 model, with detailed results provided in Appendix §E. V-E 1 Medical-oriented Reinforcement Learning Recent reasoning models such as OpenAI’s o-series and DeepSeek-R1 [ 14 ] achieve strong performance through RLVR, particularly through Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO; [ 15 ] ). RLVR encourages autonomous reasoning discovery through reward signals rather than answer memorization, addressing the limitations of SFT, such as over-reliance on supervision and limited reasoning development [ 53 ] . Motivated by this success, we extend RLVR to medical multimodal tasks. The medical dataset combines multiple sources with rigorous post-processing. First, we reformulate MCQA with word or phrase answers into open-ended QA to enhance difficulty while maintaining verifiability, leading to a data balance between MCQA and open-ended QA. Binary-answer questions ( e.g . , “Yes/No”) are downsampled to comprise 5% of the dataset to reduce bias from over-representation. Samples are then selected based on their modality and query format, aiming to ensure the data balance. Finally, a total of 100K examples are curated to form the training set. Details about the dataset usage and mixture for each stage are summarized in the Appendix § B . The detailed implementations for both Lingshu-Med (featured model) and Lingshu-Med-RL (exploratory variant) are described in Appendix § C . We analyze the potential causes from three aspects, and emphasize that
this analysis is a hypothesis rather than a fully established mechanism: 1. Vision-Reasoning Bottleneck: We hypothesize that a plausible reason for the limited gains of RLVR in the medical domain is a disconnect between textual reasoning optimization and visual perception. Unlike mathematical or coding task, where reasoning is purely symbolic and resides within the textual domain, medical VQA is a heavily visually grounded challenge. Accurate visual perception is a key prerequisite for subsequent logical deduction. Our RL implementation mainly enhances textual reasoning trajectories, but does not fundamentally improve the fine-grained visual feature extraction of the vision encoder, which may create a mismatch. For tasks requiring precise anatomical localization (SLAKE) or microscopic texture recognition (PathVQA), textual reasoning cannot compensate for visual deficits. RL tends to prioritize logically coherent, well-formatted text while overlooking ambiguous visual evidence, leading to the observed drops (-0.6, -0.8). The same effect may explain the severe degradation (-1.6) on OmniMedVQA, where distractor options are often clinically plausible, so a misread visual cue leads textual reasoning to a wrong option. In contrast, RL yields marginal gains (+0.4, +0.7) on VQA-RAD and MMMU-Med, where questions often rely on general knowledge recall once the modality or broad anatomy is identified, masking the visual bottleneck. 2. Reward Design: Our implementation adopts the prevailing accuracy-oriented, rule-based rewards, which may provide unreliable signals for open-ended medical questions with substantial linguistic variability. However, we believe this is not the main cause. On the one hand, the vast majority of our training data has definitive answers ( e.g . , multiple-choice options or medical terms). On the other hand, the model still suffers a severe degradation on the multiple-choice benchmark OmniMedVQA. 3. Data Quality: A significant proportion of medical multimodal tasks, such as lesion classification, demand visual understanding rather than complex reasoning; this distribution further exacerbates the vision-reasoning bottleneck. Moreover, the benchmarks used here are not tailored for text-centric medical reasoning, which prevents our model’s text-based reasoning abilities from being fully demonstrated. Overall, this analysis is exploratory: our observations are consistent with a vision-reasoning bottleneck but do not conclusively isolate it from factors such as reward design or data distribution. We thus present it as a hypothesis and leave rigorous verification to future work. V-G Ablation Study on Data Composition To gain insights into the impact of different data types during the medical instruction tuning stage, we conducted an ablation study by selectively removing specific categories of training data, as shown in Table V . In general, all four categories of training data outlined in § III-C , namely, {Medical, General} × \times {Multimodal, Text} , collectively contribute to Lingshu-Med’s task-solving ability, even when some data sources are not explicitly tailored for the medical domain. Notably, medical textual data emerge as the most critical component: the removal of merely 173K samples leads to substantial performance degradation on five of the seven tasks. Different types of data impact performance uniquely. Removing medical multimodal data ( - Medical Multimodal ) reduces SLAKE, PathVQA, and OmniMedVQA scores since these benchmarks focus on histopathology and X-ray images, which are prevalent in medical multimodal data. Removing general multimodal data ( - General Multimodal ) severely impacts MMMU-Med and PMC-VQA (see Appendix §G for fine-grained scale analysis). These tasks require a nuanced understanding of medical content in complex formats, such as medical charts, which are lacking in medical multimodal datasets. We conjecture that the multimodal and textual data from the general domain can effectively supplement this specialized knowledge by compensating for domain-specific limitations. This supports our hypothesis: the acquisition of comprehensive medical knowledge for multimodal problems should not be based exclusively on multimodal medical data . Rather, it requires the integration of medical text and general data for a more robust and generalizable model. Beyond the overall data composition, we examine the contributions of several high-value data components: • Medical Caption (Stage 1&2) : Skipping Stage 1&2 alignment causes moderate performance drops on PathVQA, PMC-VQA, and OmniMedVQA, suggesting that early-stage alignment remains a critical step in the training pipeline. • Medical Caption (Stage 3) : As mentioned in § III-C , we retain some high-quality medical captions during Stage 3 to mitigate local view bias. Removing this data severely degrades performance on MMMU-Med, VQA-RAD, PMC-VQA, and OmniMedVQA, underscoring the importance of caption data throughout training. • Synthetic Multimodal Medical data : Removing all synthetic multimodal data described in § II-B causes notable declines in MMMU-Med, SLAKE, PathVQA, and especially OmniMedVQA, which covers a broader range of medical modalities. This shows synthetic data is crucial for filling data gaps and enhancing medical knowledge across modalities. • Distilled Textual Medical Reasoning Data : Given the importance of textual data, we further isolate the impact of medical reasoning texts. Removing this subset causes performance drops on five of the seven tasks, nearly equivalent to removing all medical text. We attribute this effect to the dense medical knowledge encoded within CoT reasoning traces. This finding suggests a promising direction for future research: developing diverse, high-quality corpora of medical reasoning texts to systematically enhance the acquisition of medical expertise. This observation may explain the performance gains achieved through stage 2 training in Fig. 7 . Regarding instruction data, both our synthetic multimodal data and medical CoT text data demonstrate high quality across all four dimensions, which aligns with their great effectiveness in Table V . Interestingly, general multimodal data, despite not being designed for medical applications, contains a comparable amount of medical knowledge to existing medical multimodal datasets. Moreover, general multimodal data exhibits strong logical complexity, enabling the model to learn reasoning capabilities that can transfer effectively to the medical domain. VI Related work The rapid development of MLLMs has achieved tremendous improvements in the medical domain, spurring medical applications and healthcare progress [ 54 , 55 ] . Early endeavors combine LLMs with specialized medical visual encoders via a projection layer to establish vision-language alignment for medical image understanding and analysis [ 7 , 56 , 10 ] . Based on this foundation, subsequent studies adopt similar architectural paradigms for developing medical MLLMs, incorporating various strategies, such as curating more comprehensive datasets [ 57 , 8 , 6 , 58 ] , designing sophisticated training recipes [ 59 , 60 ] , employing efficient fine-tuning techniques [ 13 ] , integrating mixture-of-experts [ 51 ] , and leveraging reinforcement learning [ 16 , 17 ] , to improve performance across various medical tasks. Note that we draw high-level insights from ReasonMed [ 58 ] on the effectiveness of long-CoT and the importance of quality control; however, Lingshu-Med mainly focuses on medical multimodal understanding and reasoning, and thus requires imaging-specific synthesis and verification. Meanwhile, we do not reuse or incorporate any ReasonMed-synthesized data; our dataset and pipeline are independently built. In parallel, widely used proprietary models have integrated medical knowledge, i.e . Med-Gemini 14 14 14 https://research.google/blog/advancing-medical-ai-with-med-gemini/ and Med-PaLM [ 61 ] , and achieve strong performance on diverse medical tasks [ 62 , 63 , 64 ] . Beyond general medical MLLMs, many studies explore specialized MLLMs tailored to specific diseases or clinical contexts to better support downstream applications, such as those designed for pathology [ 65 , 66 , 12 ] , radiology [ 67 , 68 ] , ophthalmology [ 69 ] , pan-cancer analysis [ 70 ] , specific cancer types [ 71 , 72 , 73 ] , etc. Furthermore, building upon the capabilities of medical MLLMs, researchers have initiated the development of medical agents that extend model functionality through integration with external medical tools, such as AgentClinic [ 74 ] , Agent Hospital [ 75 ] , and MedAgent-Pro [ 76 ] . In contrast, we focus on developing a multimodal foundation model that accommodates diverse medical modalities and supports tasks such as question answering, diagnosis, report generation, and diverse clinical applications. VII Conclusion In this work, we introduce Lingshu-Med, a domain-specialized multimodal foundation model designed for medical AI, addressing key challenges in medical data construction , model training , and evaluation . By unifying large-scale, high-quality medical and general-domain corpora with a comprehensive data curation pipeline, encompassing image captions, QA pairs, and CoT annotations, Lingshu-Med acquires an expansive and diverse knowledge base. Our multi-stage training paradigm progressively enhances the model’s medical understanding and problem-solving skills. Moreover, we explore the potential of incorporating advanced reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards to further boost performance. Beyond model development, we emphasize the importance of evaluation standardization in the medical domain and introduce MedEvalKit, a unified framework that consolidates multiple multimodal and textual benchmarks while enforcing standardized evaluation. This toolkit enables fair, reproducible, and transparent comparisons across models. Extensive experiments confirm the superior performance of Lingshu-Med. Across a broad spectrum of medical VQA and report generation tasks, Lingshu-Med consistently surpasses open-source baselines and narrows the gap with proprietary models. Overall, Lingshu-Med and MedEvalKit provide a high-performing model, a robust evaluation toolkit, and empirically grounded guidelines on data curation, staged training, and evaluation. Appendix A Appendix A-A More Details about Data Curation A-A 1 The collected medical datasets The complete list of the collected datasets for medical multimodal data is summarized in Table VII . A-A 2 Details of data cleaning for open-sourced medical datasets Data cleaning for medical multimodal data. First, image filtering , where images with dimensions smaller than 64 64 pixels are removed to eliminate low-resolution or uninformative content. Second, image deduplication , which applies perceptual hashing with a strict Hamming distance threshold of zero to detect and remove exact duplicate images, retaining only one high-quality instance per set. To accelerate this process, we use a chunk-based deduplication technique. 15 15 15 https://github.com/xuehuachunsheng/DupImageDetection Third, text filtering , where image caption samples with fewer than 10 or more than 1024 tokens are excluded to filter out lengthy and potentially noisy examples. The full pipeline is applied to all medical image captioning datasets. For instruction data, we omit the text filtering step due to the validity of concise responses, e.g . , “yes/no”, word phrases, or short options. Image deduplication in instruction datasets specifically targets PubMedVision [ 8 ] and LLaVA-Med [ 7 ] , which exhibit a high degree of redundancy. However, cross-dataset deduplication is avoided in this case, as individual images may be paired with diverse instructions. In contrast, image deduplication across all datasets is conducted for medical captioning data. Data cleaning for medical text data. First, we apply model-based response cleaning to open-source patient-doctor dialogue datasets, such as HealthCareMagic [ 77 ] and iCliniq [ 77 ] , which are typically scraped from online healthcare platforms. These responses often contain sensitive identity information or provide explicit diagnoses and prescriptions, raising privacy and legal concerns. Specifically, we employ LLaMA-3.1-70B [ 78 ] to remove identity-related content and revise responses to avoid direct medical recommendations. Second, to eliminate redundancy, we perform text deduplication across multiple instruction datasets using min-hash locality-sensitive hashing (LSH), retaining only the highest-quality version of each near-duplicate based on the reliability of its source. Upon inspecting the medical imaging datasets included in our corpus, we found that their human-annotated meta-information can be largely grouped into four recurring label categories: (1) abnormality type , (2) tumor type , (3) anatomical structure , and (4) imaging modality . We therefore scale question templating by designing templates at the label-category level , rather than crafting dataset-specific templates. Concretely, for each label category we manually write a single seed template, and then use GPT-4o to generate 12 paraphrases that preserve the original semantics while varying wording and syntax. This yields 13 templates per category and 52 templates total (4 categories × 13 templates), which can be instantiated with the dataset-specific label values ( e.g . , particular tumor types, anatomy terms, or modalities) across the full corpus. To further ensure the faithfulness of the distilled reasoning traces, we introduce a quality-checking stage in which GPT-4o performs self-evaluation to verify consistency between the generated reasoning and the ground-truth answer, filtering out inconsistent traces. The prompt used for this quality-checking stage is shown in Table XV . Dataset usage. We collect BraTS2024 [ 109 ] , LLD-MMRI [ 96 ] , MAMA-MIA [ 97 ] and Brain-Tumor-MRI for MRI ; Mendeley Digital Knee X-ray, CheXpert [ 93 ] , and NIH-Chest-X-Ray [ 110 ] for X-ray ; AbdomenUS [ 98 ] for Ultrasound ; PAD-UFES-20 [ 111 ] for Dermoscopy ; KIPA22 [ 94 ] and DeepLesion [ 95 ] for CT ; and CPD [ 104 ] , Breast-Cancer [ 105 ] , PanNuke [ 106 ] , and EBHI-Seg [ 107 ] for Histopathology . More details of stage 1: metadata preparation. For example, in CPD [ 104 ] , we generate short captions using a staining method and nuclei score metadata with the template: “ A WSI image of carcinogenic DNA damage caused by ultraviolet (UV) radiation. The image is stained for { staining } . The relative amount of damaged nuclei (bounded in [0,1]) is { nuclei score } . ”
To minimize hallucination risk, we exclude metadata not visually inferable from the image, e.g . , patient age, based on expert consultation. All personal identifiers are removed to prevent racial or demographic biases. Additionally, we manually retrieve medical knowledge from the web to enhance the annotation process, particularly for models like GPT-4o that may lack domain-specific expertise. Thus, this stage involves metadata preparation from both the dataset and external medical references. More details of stage 2: RoI identification. RoIs are available as either segmentation masks or bounding boxes. Bounding boxes are directly overlaid on the original images, while segmentation masks are converted into bounding boxes by computing the minimal enclosing rectangles. For 3D datasets such as MAMA-MIA [ 97 ] and KIPA22 [ 94 ] , we extract 2D slices and their corresponding masks along the z-axis, and apply the same bounding box rendering procedure to generate 2D images. More details of stage 3: annotation with factual knowledge. This stage synthesizes image descriptions by integrating textual and visual information from the previous two stages.
For datasets with annotated RoIs, we input bounding-box-rendered images, short captions, and retrieved medical knowledge into GPT-4o to generate descriptive text. For datasets lacking RoI annotations, GPT-4o is prompted with the original image and metadata from Stage 1. Since visual and textual inputs from the last two stages are both manually annotated, the resulting descriptions are considered highly reliable. More details of stage 4: annotation with doctor preference. Although the generated factual descriptions are highly reliable, they are often constrained by explicitly available metadata and may overlook critical visual details beyond RoIs. To mitigate this, we consult medical professionals to identify key factors they consider when interpreting medical images. Their insights are distilled into task-specific instructions. For instance, in MRI analysis, clinicians focus on sequence type, image orientation, anatomical structures, and visible abnormalities. These instructions guide the annotation model, which, given the original image without bounding box overlays, generates alternative descriptions aligned with expert diagnostic perspectives. A-B Details of Lingshu-Med’s training stages The overview of the data mixture for the four training stages of Lingshu-Med is shown in Table XXIV . Recall that Lingshu-Med is built upon the Qwen2.5-VL model architecture [ 23 ] , which consists of three key components: a large language model (LLM), a vision encoder, and an MLP-based projector. We employ two parameter scales of Qwen2.5-VL, i.e . , 7B-Instruct and 32B-Instruct, as the foundational models. The choice of instruct versions is motivated by two primary considerations. First, these models require significantly less alignment data during training, owing to their inherent image understanding and instruction-following capabilities. Second, they support the direct use of instruction-formatted data during the alignment phase. Consequently, Lingshu-Med can adopt instruction-style data formatting from the initial training stage, which promotes consistency across the multi-stage training pipeline, reduces the overhead of additional instruction alignment, simplifies the training process, and enhances overall training stability. The initial two stages focus on constructing a robust vision-language foundation tailored to medical scenarios. Medical Shallow Alignment fine-tunes the model via a small set of medical image-text pairs, enabling it to encode medical images and generate corresponding descriptions accurately. This stage facilitates the model’s understanding of visual medical content. Medical Deep Alignment builds upon this by using larger, higher-quality, and semantically richer medical image-text pair datasets. This stage deepens the model’s domain knowledge and achieves finer-grained vision-language alignment. The subsequent stages aim to enhance the model’s utility in practical medical settings. Medical Instruction Tuning improves the model’s ability to comprehend and execute task-specific instructions across various medical use cases, thereby enhancing its generalizability to downstream tasks. Finally, Medical-oriented Reinforcement Learning incorporates the Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR; [ 15 , 16 , 17 ] ) paradigm to strengthen the model’s medical reasoning, problem-solving capacity, and interpretability. For medical shallow alignment stage, we primarily utilize the coarse caption data for training. In this case, freezing the LLM is a deliberate choice, as the coarse caption data typically consists of short and information-sparse text. Exposing LLM to such limited textual content could potentially impair its language generation capabilities. In contrast, training vision encoder and projector enables the model to better map medical visual features into LLM’s representational space. The use of coarsely annotated data is motivated by its relatively low semantic complexity, which facilitates rapid learning of the general characteristics of medical imaging modalities. This not only aids in effectively adjusting the vision encoder’s outputs for better alignment with the LLM but also contributes to faster training and improved convergence stability. For medical deep alignment stage , we unfreeze all the model parameters and utilize more high-quality image caption datasets for training. Exposure to these structured visual elements enhances the model’s ability to perform structured data interpretation and cross-modal reasoning. This is particularly beneficial in medical contexts where diagnostic reports often include laboratory tables, time-series physiological curves, and pathological maps—visual formats that differ significantly from natural images in both distribution and semantics. By learning from these general-domain examples, the model acquires transferable skills in chart interpretation and symbolic reasoning, enabling more precise analysis of abnormal physiological metrics, lesion measurements, and their temporal dynamics in clinical scenarios. For medical instruction tuning stage , in line with the prevailing practices in MLLMs [ 7 , 8 , 3 , 26 ] , the medical instruction-tuning stage is designed to refine MLLM’s instruction following capability with a broad spectrum of task directives, ensuring that its outputs are accurately aligned with user intentions. To accommodate a wide breadth of clinical use cases, we unlock all parameters and perform large-scale, end-to-end optimization of the entire model. The training corpus transcends conventional instruction formats, such as image descriptions, question-answer pairs, and multiple-choice questions, by integrating an extensive suite of scenario-oriented queries collected and synthesized from multiple sources. These queries span diagnosis, clinical examination, medical knowledge retrieval, clinical report generation, and anatomical structure localization. This breadth markedly augments the model’s domain competency. In addition, we enrich the corpus with carefully vetted high-quality general-domain data and medical text data, both of which have proven effective in enhancing performance on downstream medical tasks. Incorporating such data is capable of counterbalancing the inherent image-centric information bias of image-text pair data, expanding the model’s conceptual scope beyond visual content, and fostering a more holistic grasp of medical knowledge. Moreover, our training data comprises advanced and complex data formats, such as multi-image reasoning tasks, multi-turn dialogues, and queries that require detailed reasoning processes, which demand deeper analytical acuity. Collectively, this diversified and sophisticated training data equips the MLLM to navigate complex clinical scenarios with enhanced precision and analytical capabilities. For medical-oriented RL stage, recent advancements in reasoning models, exemplified by OpenAI’s o-series and DeepSeek-R1 [ 14 ] , have set new benchmarks in complex tasks through advanced post-training strategies. Central to these improvements is reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR), with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO; [ 15 ] ) emerging as a prominent method due to its efficiency and effectiveness. It has been increasingly adopted for training MLLMs to enhance reasoning capabilities [ 112 , 113 , 114 ] . Building on RLVR’s success, some work applies it to medical domains for improving generalizability, interpretability, and reliability [ 16 , 17 ] . Conventional SFT encounters key limitations: 1) over-reliance on answer supervision, which can cause overfitting and shortcut learning, particularly detrimental in medical scenarios; and 2) limited promotion of deliberate reasoning skills. RLVR addresses these issues by encouraging models to autonomously discover reasoning pathways through reward signals, rather than relying on answer memorization or teacher-guided CoT imitation [ 53 ] . Accordingly, we adopt GRPO to train Lingshu-Med, leveraging a carefully curated medical verifiable dataset. The medical verifiable dataset is derived from multiple sources (Table XXIV ) and undergoes rigorous post-processing. Since most collected samples are in multiple-choice format, we reformulate those with word or phrase answers as open-ended questions to enhance difficulty while maintaining verifiability. This process also leads to a data balance between MCQA and open-ended QA. Additionally, binary-answer questions ( e.g . , “Yes”/“No”) are downsampled to approximately 5% of the dataset to mitigate potential bias from their over-representation. Samples are then selected based on their modality and query format, aiming to ensure the data balance. A total of 100K examples are curated to form the training set for the RL stage. For reward design, we follow common practices [ 14 , 115 ] by using a strict format reward and an accuracy reward, with respective weights of 0.5 and 1 . A-C Implementation Details As previously outlined, Lingshu-Med builds upon two variants of the Qwen2.5-VL-Instruct model, with parameter sizes of 7B and 32B, and is further optimized via continual training. The standard training process is organized into three primary stages, which leads to our Lingshu-Med. Across all stages, we adopt the AdamW optimizer with cosine learning rate scheduler and a warm-up step of 100 . The maximum sequence length is set to 8,192 tokens, per-device training batch size is configured as 1 , and gradient accumulation step is set to 8 . In Medical Shallow Alignment stage, LLM remains frozen, only the vision encoder and the projection layer are fine-tuned for one epoch, with learning rates set to 2e-6 and 1e-5 , respectively. During the Medical Deep Alignment and Medical Instruction Tuning stages, we unfreeze the LLM and fine-tune it with a learning rate of 1e-5 , while maintaining the same learning rates for the vision encoder and projector. These stages are trained for one and two epochs, respectively. To enhance training efficiency, we employ data packing during the Medical Instruction Tuning stage. Preliminary experiments suggest that data packing at this stage does not adversely affect model performance. However, similar attempts in Medical Shallow and Deep Alignment stages have led to a marked decline in model performance. This decline is likely attributable to the high prevalence of short textual samples within the medical image-caption dataset. 35 35 35 Although we synthesize a certain amount of long-form captions, it constitutes only a small proportion of the overall dataset. When packed into longer sequences, these short entries contribute sparse gradients during normalization, undermining the training dynamics. Additionally, data packing substantially reduces the number of training steps, which may hinder sufficient convergence. Extending the number of epochs to compensate could result in overfitting. Consequently, we opt not to use data packing during the Medical Shallow and Deep Alignment stages. For the Medical-oriented RL stage, we initialize training from Lingshu-Med checkpoint and apply GRPO for one epoch using the AdamW optimizer. The maximum sequence length is set to 4096 , with a rollout batch size of 512 and a global batch size of 128 . The learning rate is configured at 1e-6 , the sampling temperature at 1.0 , the KL divergence loss coefficient at 1e-3 , and 16 responses are sampled for each prompt. For reward design, we follow common practices [ 14 , 115 ] by using a strict format reward and an accuracy reward, with respective weights of 0.5 and 1 . The resultant model is named as Lingshu-Med-RL. A-D More Details about MedEvalKit The rapid progress of medical MLLMs has yielded impressive performance across various tasks. However, the absence of a unified evaluation framework has led to inconsistencies in performance comparison across models. Additionally, reproducing or deploying existing models in a standardized environment often requires substantial time and computational resources. A systematic and rigorous evaluation across diverse medical tasks is essential for accurately assessing model capabilities, identifying strengths and limitations, and providing actionable feedback for further development. Reproducible, quantitative evaluation is thus vital to advancing the optimization of medical MLLMs. An effective evaluation of medical MLLMs requires a multiple-dimensional assessment, including medical knowledge, visual understanding, and reasoning ability. While existing frameworks, such as LMMs-Eval [ 27 ] , Eval-Harness, 36 36 36 https://zenodo.org/records/12608602 and VLMEvalKit [ 28 ] , can be applied to evaluate medical MLLMs, they are primarily designed for general multimodal tasks. As a result, they exhibit limitations in terms of medical data coverage and model adaptation, making them difficult to apply directly. This not only reduces evaluation efficiency but may also introduce potential biases. To comprehensively and efficiently evaluate the performance of medical MLLMs, we develop a systematic evaluation framework, MedEvalKit, which integrates mainstream medical benchmarks and task types, supporting a variety of question formats, including multiple-choice, closed-ended, and open-ended questions as well as medical report generation. It covers representative tasks in medical multimodal and textual understanding, offering broad applicability. MedEvalKit accommodates both multimodal and text-only inputs, enabling unified evaluation of both medical MLLMs and LLMs. To enhance standardization and reproducibility, we standardized data preprocessing formats and post-processing protocols. A consistent model deployment and inference interface is provided to support rapid integration and one-click evaluation. For result assessment, MedEvalKit incorporates a dual verification mechanism that combines rule-based evaluation with LLM-as-a-Judge strategy, integrating both objective and subjective assessment to improve evaluation stability and reliability. Additionally, MedEvalKit supports inference acceleration via vLLM [ 29 ] , enabling high-throughput and parallel evaluation, with strong scalability and engineering usability. A-F Physician Evaluation Setup Raters. The evaluation was conducted by two board-certified radiologists (an associate chief radiologist and a senior attending radiologist) with 12 and 7 years of post-residency clinical experience, respectively. All cases in both the medical diagnosis and report generation tasks were graded by these radiologists, ensuring that the radiology reports under evaluation were assessed by domain-matched experts. Neither rater was involved in model development, and both were blinded to model identities throughout the study. Rating Procedure. We randomly sampled 50 cases from each of the medical diagnosis and medical report generation test sets. For each case, responses were generated by three models (Lingshu-Med, GPT-4.1, and Qwen2.5-VL) and strictly anonymised before annotation: model identities were concealed and responses were randomly assigned as Model-1, Model-2, and Model-3 to eliminate any potential source bias. Physicians rated each response independently on the following six dimensions using a 1–5 integer scale, where higher scores indicate better performance: • Clinical Relevance : Whether the response captures the key clinical features of the case without omitting critical information. • Clinical Actionability : Whether the proposed management plan is appropriate and provides clinically valuable guidance. • Safety : Whether the response contains potentially misleading or dangerous information, hallucinated content, or recommendations that could delay or misdirect clinical care. • Completeness : Whether the response is comprehensive and free of significant omissions. • Clarity : Whether the response is clearly expressed, logically coherent, and easy to follow. • Professionalism : Whether medical terminology is used accurately and the response adheres to accepted clinical conventions. Score aggregation and inter-rater agreement. The final score of each response on each dimension is the mean of the two radiologists’ independent ratings. Because each response was rated on an ordinal 1–5 scale, we quantified rating consistency with the quadratic-weighted Cohen’s κ \kappa , which penalises larger disagreements more heavily. Agreement was substantial across all six dimensions (mean κ = 0.64 \kappa=0.64 , range 0.59 0.59 – 0.68 0.68 ) [ 116 ] . In addition, the two radiologists assigned identical scores in 36% of responses and agreed within ± 1 \pm 1 point in 81%.
